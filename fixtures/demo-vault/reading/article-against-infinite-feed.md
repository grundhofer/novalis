# Article: Against the Infinite Feed

A polemic against bottomless scrolling that spends most of its length on a subtler target:
interfaces with no natural end state. The author's claim is that a list which can always
produce one more item removes the moment where you'd otherwise decide you were done, and that
this — not addictiveness in any pharmacological sense — is the mechanism worth designing
against.

## Takeaways

- Finishing is a designed event. Inboxes that can empty and books that end give you a stopping
  cue for free; feeds deliberately don't.
- The essay's best move is noting that this applies to work tools too — a backlog with no
  bottom produces the same low-grade unease as a social feed.
- Pagination gets a partial defence here, which I didn't expect to find persuasive.

## What I'd apply

Our task views scroll forever, and I've never once felt finished in one. A day view with a
hard boundary — these items, this day, nothing below — is a small change with a real effect on
whether the tool feels closable. Related to the stopping-cue material in
[[Article: What Latency Costs Attention]] and to the browsing-without-an-end problem in
[[Search vs Browse]].
