# The Quiet Machine

Okonkwo's history of maintenance work — bridge inspectors, hospital sterilisation teams, the
people who keep municipal water systems running — argued as a design book rather than a
labour one. Her claim is that we design almost exclusively for the moment of installation and
then act surprised when systems degrade on a schedule we could have predicted.

## Takeaways

- Anything with a maintenance cost that nobody owns will be quietly abandoned, no matter how
  well it was built. Ownership is a design property.
- The best-run systems in her case studies are the ones that make their own decay *visible*
  early and cheaply, rather than the ones that fail least often.
- Maintenance knowledge lives in people, not documents, and evaporates within about eighteen
  months of a team changing.

## What I'd apply

The visible-decay idea is the one I keep returning to. Our vault has three folders nobody has
opened since spring and no signal that says so. A "last touched" surface — not a nag, just an
honest display — would tell me which parts of my own system I've already abandoned. That's
the same instinct as the honest failure reporting in
[[Offline-First UX]], and it argues against the tidy-forever fantasy
in [[The Gardener and the Archivist]].
